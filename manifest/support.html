Answers to the questions that come up, in the order they usually come up. Anything not covered here: rainbow@svrainbow.net.

Getting started

What does Rainbow’s End Manifest actually do?

It keeps the whole book of your boat in one place. From your vessel’s particulars it derives the U.S. Coast Guard safety-equipment checklist for your boat — the right items, current terminology, correct citations — and lets you record where each piece is stowed. From that, it generates a passenger safety briefing for your boat, not a generic one. It carries a daily cruising log with voyages, running engine-hour totals, the miles under her keel, and fuel and water estimates, a maintenance schedule filtered to the systems you actually have — due by the calendar or the engine-hour meter, whichever comes first — and a declarable Inventory for clearing in abroad. It works offline, with optional sync through your own iCloud.

What should I set up first?

Her manifest. Open the book and tap Edit boat: her name and papers, the owners and people aboard, dimensions (LOA drives the equipment class), propulsion, heads, generator and A/C, propane, shore power and renewables, batteries, tanks with capacities, burn rates, the water maker, and the engine and generator hour-meter readings your running hours start from. Ten minutes once, and every page reshapes itself around your answers — change LOA or add a gasoline generator and watch the Gear list redraw.

The book

Can I add a photo of my boat — and describe her?

Yes. Edit boat’s Vessel section takes her hull color, her identifying features (rig, canvas, davits — what a searcher or a guest would spot), and a photo, chosen through the system picker. They print on Her papers in the book — the description a float plan, a search party, or a port captain asks for. The photo is deliberately kept small (about 800 px): at that size it syncs through your iCloud, rides along in Export the book, and shows on every device — a description aid, not a wallpaper.

What is the book on the Boat tab?

The ship’s book: a leather cover that opens to paper pages — The Boat, Gear, Checklists, Log, Maintenance, and the three ledgers. How it reads follows the width in your hands, not the device. Held broad — an iPad or a larger iPhone turned to landscape — the sections are leather index tabs down the right edge and the book opens flat into a two-page spread. Held narrow, the pages run full width, and the section name at the foot of the page opens The Index — the book’s contents page; tap any section to jump there. Pages turn like a logbook’s either way (tap Next page, the back chevron, or swipe). Every page reads from your real records — nothing in the book is a picture.

How do the book and the Editor relate?

The book is for reviewing the record and making the day’s entries — reading her papers, checking what’s aboard and where it’s stowed, giving the briefing, logging a quick day, a fill, work done, or a cost. The Editor is full data entry: complete forms, every field, scrolling lists, search, with its own bar for moving between topics — and The Book, leftmost on that bar, brings you home. Only one shows at a time; open the Editor from the leather tab hanging at the top of every page (it’s also the last row of the index). For a single quick edit the book floats the editor over the page instead — Edit boat on the Boat pages, Log the gear on the Gear pages, the fill editor in the Fuel Log — and Done returns you to your page. Nothing exists in only one place by accident: the book reviews; the Editor edits.

The checklist

Is the Gear list the law?

The federal items are the federal minimums — 33 CFR and 46 CFR carriage requirements for recreational vessels, each with its citation. Two honest caveats, stated in the app as well: state law can add requirements on its own waters, and a checklist is preparation for — never a substitute for — the free Coast Guard Auxiliary Vessel Safety Check. Pass one and she wears the decal.

Why does the app say “wearable PFD” and “5-B” instead of “Type II” and “B-I”?

Because the regulations changed. Since 2014 the rules classify personal flotation devices (PFDs) as wearable or throwable, and new labels use performance levels (Level 70 is the everyday wearable). Since April 2022 extinguishers count by UL rating — 5-B and 20-B — and disposable bottles expire 12 years from their stamped manufacture date. Old gear with old labels stays legal while serviceable; the app teaches the current words.

Why did an item appear or disappear from my list?

The list is derived, not fixed. Length class, propulsion, fuel, coastal versus inland waters, heads, a fixed fire system, even a gasoline generator on a diesel boat — each changes what the law requires. If something moved, something on your Boat tab changed.

What is the Inventory page?

Her standing declaration for clearing into another country, at the end of the Gear section: a firearms list (even “none” — that answer is itself the declaration), equipment and electronics with serials for temporary-import permits, dutiable alcohol and tobacco, medications, foodstuffs for biosecurity, and animals. Record it once in the Editor’s Gear tab; Share the list hands an official the whole thing as plain text — AirDrop it, print it, or simply show the page.

The briefing

Why does the briefing show “location not recorded”?

The briefing reads from the Gear tab. Check an item aboard and enter where it’s stowed — “port cockpit locker” — and the briefing’s “Where things live” section says exactly that to your guests. The MAYDAY card fills in your boat’s name, call sign, and MMSI from the Boat tab.

The log

What is “Before you go”?

The skipper’s predeparture checklist, in the book’s Checklists section — its landing page offers it beside the passenger briefing, and the day’s-entry page links to both. It is drawn from your manifest, not a laminated generic: a gasoline inboard gets the blower, a diesel gets oil and cooling water, a generator brings its own strainer, a sailboat gets her rig and jacklines, a paddle craft keeps the short list. The lines print what the book already knows — the fuel and water estimates, the flare and extinguisher dates from Maintenance. Checks belong to today and reset with the date; when you log the day, the entry offers to note the tally in the journal.

What are the quick links at the top of the first page?

The day’s work, one tap from where the book opens: Log the day, Log a fill, Log the work, and Log a cost each turn straight to that section’s entry page. The rest of the page is her papers — and the fleet row beneath Edit boat switches boats mid-read.

What’s a voyage? What’s a lay day?

Start a voyage — right on the day’s-entry page or in the Editor — name it, and every entry, legs and lay days alike, collects under it until you end it. A lay day is a day aboard without getting under way (at anchor, in a marina away from home): it keeps the water estimate honest and can log generator hours. Past voyages sit collapsed with their totals — days, legs, nautical miles, engine hours — and expand for review.

How do I log a passage of a day or more?

Log the departure as you leave. On the day’s entry, choose Underway, fill in the date, departure time, where from and where to, the engine hours and the persons aboard, and tap Log the departure. The entry is saved as under way, and until you arrive every day at sea counts toward the voyage’s days and the water estimate.

When you get in, tap Arrived at the top of the day’s entry. The same entry opens with the arrival day already set: add the arrival time and engine hours, take the miles the watches logged, and tap Log the arrival. You can also log a whole passage at once after arriving; the Arrives stepper under the times sets the day she got in.

How do I log the watches on an overnight passage?

At each change of watch, tap Change of watch on the day’s entry (or Log the watch atop Her papers while a voyage or passage is under way). Write the time in local time or UTC and the page shows the other. Fill from GPS takes the boat’s position, or type it as you would on a chart (29 18.4 N, 94 47.2 W). Add course and speed, the wind, the sea, the barometer (millibars or inches, or tap to read the phone’s own barometer), the sky, the sails, the engine, and who kept the watch. Wind, sea, sky, sails and engine carry over from the last watch; change what’s changed.

From the second position on, the page works out the distance, speed and course made good since the last fix. Leave course and speed blank and it logs those, marked “made good.” The barometer’s three-hour trend reads in the forecasts’ words, from steady to falling very rapidly, and a quick fall is flagged. Watches never add miles or hours to the voyage; when you arrive, tap Arrived on the day’s entry, and the passage’s form offers the miles the watches logged between departure and arrival.

How do engine hours and the miles under her keel work?

Enter each meter’s reading on the Editor’s Boat page and the day’s entry pre-fills “Engine hrs — start” with the running total; log the finish reading and the total moves forward. Correct the figure on the Boat page any time and the total restarts from the number you just entered. The miles under her keel work the same way: give the book the miles she’d sailed when your log began, and every logged leg adds on top — her lifetime total, on the log page.

What happened to sail hours?

Retired, deliberately. Duration minus engine hours claimed she was sailing whenever the engine was off — but a lunch stop on the hook is not sailing. The log now shows the leg’s honest duration and engine hours, and claims nothing more.

How do I find an old trip?

The search box atop the log matches voyage names, places, weather, journal text, and dates — written either way: “2026-08” and “aug 21” both find the day. While searching, matching voyages open to just their matching days, with the voyage totals still describing the whole voyage.

How does the fuel estimate work — and how much should I trust it?

It’s arithmetic, and it says so. A sailboat gives one motoring burn rate, and rate times logged engine hours draws each tank down. A powerboat burns very differently by how she’s driven, so she gives three figures instead — total burn, all engines together, at trawling speed, fast cruise, and wide-open throttle — and each day’s entry logs the hours at each speed (the three should total the engine hours for the leg; the form checks and says so). Each engine still keeps its own meter — skippers run one engine or the other — and the estimate splits the day’s fuel across tanks by each engine’s share of the hours. Fills add; a fill marked topped off — or a measured amount entered from the dipstick or gauge — calibrates the estimate, and the math continues from there. Until a tank has been topped off or measured once, the app says “unknown” rather than guessing — and if hours are logged at a speed whose rate isn’t set, it likewise refuses to guess. The gauge always outranks the arithmetic. Fresh water works the same way, boat-wide, drawn down per logged day at your per-person rate unless an entry records its own figure.

Why are there two “persons” numbers?

The Gear page asks for the maximum persons she’s equipped for — that number sizes the gear (one PFD per person she might carry) and never changes with the weekend. Each log entry records the persons actually aboard for that trip, and the water estimate runs on that day’s logged crew. Days logged without a crew count fall back to the equipped-for maximum.

Maintenance

What are the three ledgers?

The books of what she costs — each with its own place in the ship’s book (a fore-edge tab when the book is wide, a row in The Index when it’s narrow), and each in the same shape: a summary page (totals, then the record) and an entry page behind its button. The Fuel Log: every fill — date, amount, price, and the dock — which also feeds the fuel-aboard estimate. The Work Log: everything done to the boat — repairs, routine maintenance, equipment added or upgraded — who did it, where, and what it cost. The Ownership Log: what she costs at rest — dockage and storage, insurance, registration and taxes, utilities, dues. The Maintenance tab keeps shortcut rows to all three, with the tally: totals by month, year, and category. Entries without a price still belong in their logs; they just don’t tally.

What is “Needs the skipper’s attention”?

The 60-day lookahead: every dated item that’s expired, overdue, or falls due within the next 60 days, sorted soonest first, each row saying how many days out or past it stands. Engine and generator items on a tracked meter can also raise the alarm by hours — a row that reads “30 hrs past” got there by the hour meter, not the calendar. It reads from the same records as the schedule below it, so entering a date once does both jobs. Unrecorded items don’t alarm — the schedule rows themselves show “Not recorded.”

Does the schedule track engine hours, or just dates?

Both, once the app knows your meters. Engine and generator service items that carry an hour interval — oil at 100 hrs, fuel filters at 250, and so on — watch the running engine-hour totals kept by the log. Record a service date and the app stamps the hour-meter reading beside it (correct it if the work happened at a different reading); from then on the item comes due at whichever arrives first, the months or the hours — exactly what “100 hrs or annually” has always meant. A boat that sat all winter gets the calendar nag; a boat that crossed the Gulf in May gets the hour nag. If you haven’t set the hour-meter readings on the Editor’s Boat page, nothing changes — the schedule runs on the calendar alone, as before. Shared items like the Racor separators score against the hardest-working engine’s meter.

Where do the intervals come from?

The mainstream conventions — engine-manufacturer schedules, marine-industry practice, and the verified document cycles (USCG documentation renewal, FCC ship station license, NOAA EPIRB registration, flare and extinguisher expirations). Items marked Hard date are legal or safety expirations; past the date, the gear doesn’t count. Everything else is a service interval, and your owner’s manuals always win.

Why does my boat’s maintenance list differ from my friend’s?

Same reason as the Gear list: it’s derived. Twin engines get port and starboard lines; each Racor-type separator gets its own line; a generator, air conditioning, a sea chest, a composting head each bring their own items; sailboats get the rig category and powerboats don’t.

Housekeeping

Where is my data? Is there an account?

No account and no server of ours — everything lives on your device, and nothing ever reaches us. The one optional exception is iCloud sync (below), which keeps a copy in your own iCloud, readable only by your Apple ID. See the privacy policy, which is short because there is little to disclose. Deleting the app deletes the data on that device; a synced copy stays in your iCloud until you remove it.

What happens if I lose or replace my phone?

Three safety nets now. If iCloud sync is on, a new device signed into your Apple ID picks the fleet up from your iCloud as soon as you switch sync on there. Beyond that: an iCloud Backup (or an encrypted computer backup) of your device includes the app’s data like any other app’s — restore a new phone from backup, or migrate with Apple’s Quick Start, and the whole book comes with it. And Export the book on the Boat page writes everything — every boat, her gear, dates, and log — to one file you control: save it anywhere, and Import a book brings it back. A phone lost with none of the three takes its data with it, so export after a good voyage the way you’d photograph a paper logbook.

Does it sync between my iPhone and iPad?

Yes — through your own iCloud, and only if you ask. In Edit boat, under iCloud sync, switch on Sync her books across your devices on each device. Every device signed into the same Apple ID then keeps the same fleet: edits push a few seconds after the pen lifts and arrive when the other device next opens the app (or on Sync now). The copy lives in your iCloud’s private database — no account with us, no server of ours, and we cannot read it. The privacy policy said it would describe any online feature plainly before it shipped; its iCloud sync section is that description.

Two devices edited the same boat — who wins?

The newer edit keeps that boat’s whole book. Sync merges boat by boat: whichever device wrote in a boat’s book most recently carries her, entirely — it does not stitch two books’ edits together line by line. In practice the window is small because devices sync within seconds of an edit; the habit that makes it a non-issue is finishing your entry on one device before picking up the other. Different boats never collide, and a boat deleted on one device stays deleted — unless the other edited her afterward, in which case the edit wins and she’s back aboard.

Does it work with Rainbow’s End Anchoring?

They’re siblings from the same shop and share the family look, but each stands alone. Anchoring sizes and manages your ground tackle; Manifest keeps the book of the boat. The Gear tab will happily remind you that an anchor is not federally required equipment — and that prudence disagrees.
