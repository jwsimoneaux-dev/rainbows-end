How Anchoring works it out

Every figure Rainbow’s End Anchoring gives you, and where it comes from. Each section shows the formula, what it means, what it rests on, and the numbers for Rainbow, the ketch the app starts with, so you can check them in the app yourself.

This page describes version 2.2.0, now in testing. Until it reaches the App Store, the version there differs in four ways: it does not take a current, it draws the chain’s curve from its weight in air rather than in water, it uses one chain weight for every grade, and it cannot mark the anchor after the drop. Every other figure here is the same.

Rainbow: 46.7 ft long, 13.7 ft beam, 5.5 ft draft, 36,500 lb, bow roller 6 ft above the water, a 65 lb scoop anchor on 200 ft of 3/8″ G43 chain. The night: 10 ft on the sounder, 12 kt of wind, a short chop, soft mud, overnight in settled weather, one anchor down.

Depth

How deep the rode has to reach

Scope is measured from the bow roller to the seabed at the worst of the night, not from the surface. In shallow water the height of the roller is a large share of it.

Depth to reach = sounder + correction + bow roller + tide to rise + surge correction: added when your sounder reads from the transducer or the keel · surge: 0 flat, 0.5 ft chop, 1.5 ft moderate swell, 3 ft rough

Scope

How much rode to pay out

A base ratio for the kind of rode, raised for wind, for a stop you will leave the boat on, for doubtful ground and for boats that range at anchor, and lowered for a short stop with someone aboard.

Scope = base + wind + stop + ground + boat + shallow base: 4:1 all chain, 5.5:1 chain and rope, 7:1 mostly rope · wind: +0.5 from 15 kt, +1 from 20, +1.5 from 25, +2.5 from 30, +4 from 40, sized halfway to the gusts, and in a current on the wind that pulls as hard as wind and current together (see Current) · stop: −1 a short stop, +0.5 unsettled, +1 unattended, +2 storm · ground: +0.5 soft mud, grass or unknown · boat: +0.5 a catamaran, +0.5 a high-windage motor boat · shallow: +0.5 under 15 ft to reach · never under 4:1 (3:1 a short stop), never over 12:1, and 10:1 at least in a storm

Pay out = scope × depth to reach. The chain’s curve is checked separately below; it adds advice, never a longer pay-out.

The load

How hard the wind pulls

The wind pushes on everything the boat shows it from ahead: the hull above the water, the rig, and the canvas. The pull grows with the square of the wind, so twice the wind is four times the pull.

F = q × Cd × A × V² q = 0.00339 lb per sq ft per knot² (air) · Cd = 1.1 for a monohull, 1.2 a catamaran, +0.05 a motor boat · A = frontal area, sq ft · V = wind, knots
Physics the drag equationOur estimate the frontal area and Cd

The frontal area is estimated from what you enter: the hull from beam and bow height, the rig from length and rig type, the canvas from how much you carry. A boat with a hard dodger, davits and a dinghy can show half again what the estimate gives.

Hull: 13.7 ft beam × 6 ft bow height × 0.6553.4 sq ft
Rig: a ketch’s two masts and furled sails125.1 sq ft
Canvas: a bimini and furling headsail (moderate)21.4 sq ft
Frontal area199.9 sq ft
Wind force at 12 kt: 0.00339 × 1.1 × 199.9 × 12²107 lb

From steady wind to the design load

Wind alone is not what breaks ground tackle. Waves surge the boat back on her rode, and a boat that sails about at anchor snatches at it. The app multiplies the wind’s pull for both.

Design load = F × surge × yaw × style + current surge 1.15 flat · 1.6 chop · 2.1 moderate swell · 2.8 rough — yaw 1.15 a monohull, 1.35 a catamaran, +0.1 a motor boat; a riding sail keeps about a third of the yaw above 1 (1.0525 for a monohull), on one anchor or a mooring ball only, and not above 40 kt or in a current of a knot or more — style 1.15 Med moored, 1.3 lines ashore · the peak load uses the gusts

Current

How hard the stream pulls

Water is about 836 times denser than air. Head-on, a hull is streamlined and meets little of it. Lying at an angle to the stream, her whole underwater profile meets it. So the pull has two parts, and the angle matters more than the speed.

Along the hull: qw × V² × S × Cf × 1.2 qw = 2.835 lb per sq ft per knot² in seawater, 2.763 in fresh · S = wetted surface, by the Denny–Mumford formula, 1.7 × waterline length × draft + displaced volume ÷ draft · Cf = 0.075 ÷ (log Re − 2)², the ITTC 1957 friction line · 1.2 for a full-bodied hull
Across the hull: qw × V² × 1.0 × Alat × sin²β Alat = the underwater profile seen from abeam · β = the angle she lies to the stream: 15° in a river, 30° in a tidal stream, which the wind turns her across half the time
Physics water friction and crossflowPublished the ITTC 1957 line; the Denny–Mumford formulaOur estimate the areas and the angle

We take the waterline as 0.85 of her length and the profile from abeam as 0.65 of waterline × draft, keel included. A current is steady, so surge and yaw multiply the wind only.

Wetted surface475 sq ft
Underwater profile from abeam142 sq ft

How the current raises the scope

The scope rule sizes on wind. In a current it sizes on the wind that would pull as hard as the wind and the current together, through the same table, so the same limits hold.

Wind to size on = √(wind² + current ÷ k) k = her design pull per knot² of wind, with surge and yaw · the current’s pull is steady, the wind’s grows with its square

The chain’s curve

When the chain pulls straight

Chain hangs in a curve under its own weight, so the pull at the anchor stays along the bottom. Pull hard enough and the curve comes out; above that load the chain no longer cushions anything, and the snubber does.

Load at which the chain pulls straight = w × (L² − d²) ÷ (2d) w = the chain’s weight in water per foot · L = chain out · d = depth to reach
Chain to keep the curve at a load T = √(d² + 2 × d × T ÷ w)
Physics the catenaryPublished chain weights, ACCO chain catalog

Steel in water weighs 0.87 of its weight in air (the water it displaces). The weight in air is the chain maker’s figure for your size and grade. The app offers more chain only when it is cheap: never more than 1.3 times what is out.

Holding and the margin

How hard the anchor holds, and how much to spare

Once set, an anchor holds roughly in proportion to its weight, by a factor that depends on its design and the bottom. Whether it sets at all is a separate question, which the app asks separately.

Holding = anchor weight × holding factor the factor by design and bottom: a scoop anchor 45 in hard sand, 18 in soft mud; a fluke anchor 50 in hard sand, 4 in grass; and so on for seven designs and ten bottoms
Margin = holding ÷ design load wanted: 1.5 a short stop · 2 overnight, settled · 2.5 unsettled · 3 unattended · 4 a storm

Strength of the gear

What breaks first

The system is as strong as its weakest part. Chain size sets the curve; chain grade sets the strength. The app keeps the two apart and checks the peak load against the weakest part.

Safe load: chain = its working load limit · rope = breaking strength ÷ 5
Published chain working load limits, ACCO chain catalogOur estimate the rope’s 5 to 1

A chain’s working load limit already carries the maker’s own margin. Rope gets five to one because anchor rode is rarely new and rarely inspected end to end.

The swing

How far she can range

She can lie anywhere on a circle around the anchor. Its radius is how far the rode reaches across the bottom, plus the boat herself.

Radius = √(L² − d²) + LOA L = rode paid out · d = depth to reach · LOA = length overall
Geometry the rode taken straight

Taking the rode as straight is the safe side: the sag of the chain only ever shortens its reach.

Reach across the bottom: √(78.75² − 17.5²)76.8 ft
Swing radius: 76.8 + 46.7123.5 ft

Mooring balls

How far a ball lets her range

The ball floats on a riser from the mooring’s anchor. Laid for high water, the riser is slackest at low water, which is when the ball can range furthest.

Ball’s reach = √(riser² − low-water depth²) · riser = ratio × high-water depth ratio: 3:1 chain to a block, 1.5:1 a helix · pennant: 2.5 × bow height, at least 10 ft · swing = ball’s reach + pennant + LOA
Geometry the reachOur estimate the riser ratios and the pennant, from harbor practice

A long riser ranges further, so the app takes the top of the usual range for chain. It does not rank your own anchors on a ball; none of them is in the water.

The anchor watch’s fence

The line the alarm watches

The fence is the swing circle with room for GPS scatter. It allows for the phone anywhere aboard, since the swing already includes the boat’s length.

Fence = swing radius + 50 ft a held boat: half her length + 50 ft · a Bahamian moor: the corridor + 50 ft · a ball: twice the ball’s reach and pennant, + LOA, + 50 ft
Our estimate the 50 ft margin

You can move the fence in steps of 5%. It cannot be tightened inside the swing, which she reaches by doing nothing wrong, or widened past 200%.

One anchor: 123.5 + 50173.5 ft
Tightest step: 123.5 of 173.5, to the next 5%75%

The alarm and the early warning

When the alarm sounds

One stray GPS fix must not wake you. The alarm sounds after 3 fixes in a row clearly outside the fence: outside by more than 15 ft, or half the fix’s own accuracy if that is more. It stands down by itself when she comes back inside.

The early warning is a band inside the fence, 50 ft wide (a held boat’s 25). It chimes once as she enters it, and not again until she has come 15 ft back inside. When you widen the fence, the plan’s own line warns too. A band narrower than 10 ft is no band, and the screen says so.

Marking the anchor after the drop

When the alarm was not set at the drop

From the bow, backing down gently with the rode straight, the anchor lies the rode’s reach ahead, along the rode. The direction is the weak point, so the fence is widened by how far the mark may be off.

Anchor = here + reach, along the bearing · widened by max(reach × sin e, 5% of reach) reach: the rode’s shape at 300 lb · e: 10° by the phone’s compass, 15° typed from the chartplotter · reach error 5%
