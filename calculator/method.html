How the Boat Calculator works it out

Rainbow, a Perry 47 ketch: 46′ 7″ long, 36.8 ft on the waterline, 13′ 6″ beam, 30,400 lb empty, with 14,000 lb of encapsulated iron ballast in a shoal-draft keel. Sail area 973 ft²: main, 100% fore triangle and mizzen. As she sails: 250 gal of diesel, 125 gal of water, and 2,000 lb of people, stores and gear.

The load

What she carries that the brochure left out

A builder’s displacement is usually the boat empty. Every ratio below is worked twice: on the published figure, and on the boat as she sails. Compare boats in the same state of load.

Pounds per inch

How far the load sinks her

The weight that sinks her one inch, from the area of her waterplane. The waterplane is estimated from her waterline length and beam.

Sail area to displacement

How much sail she carries for her weight

A sailor’s power-to-weight ratio. It says nothing about how well the hull uses the power.

SA/D = sail area ÷ (displacement ÷ 64)^⅔ under 15 under-canvassed · 15–18 good performance · 18–20 excellent performance · over 20 a handful
Published the formula and the bands
Empty: 973 ÷ (30,400 ÷ 64)^⅔16.0
As she sails: 973 ÷ (35,218 ÷ 64)^⅔14.5

Empty, she is in the good-performance band. Loaded, she drops just under 15. Her published sheet gives 16.

Ralph Naranjo, “Measuring Performance,” Practical Sailor, 2020.

Ballast ratio

How much of her weight is ballast

How much the ballast helps her stand up depends on how deep it sits. Two boats with the same ratio can stand up very differently. Iron is bulkier than lead, so the same weight sits higher in the keel.

Ballast ratio = ballast ÷ displacement × 100
Geometry the ratio
Empty: 14,000 ÷ 30,40046.1%
As she sails: 14,000 ÷ 35,21839.8%

Depth of ballast: Ralph Naranjo, “Measuring Performance,” Practical Sailor, 2020.

Comfort ratio

How quickly she moves upward in a seaway

Ted Brewer devised it as a measure of motion comfort: how fast a boat moves upward in waves. Lower is quicker and less comfortable; higher is calmer. Brewer put moderate, successful ocean cruisers in the low to mid 30s.

It ignores wave direction, hull shape, bow buoyancy, ballast depth and speed. It does not measure safety. Use it to compare boats of a similar size and style.

Comfort = displacement ÷ (0.65 × (0.7 LWL + 0.3 LOA) × beam^1.333)
Published the formula and Brewer’s range
Empty36.6
As she sails42.4

Jamie Gifford, “Hands-on Sailor: Comfort Ratio,” Cruising World, 2026, quoting Ted Brewer. The exponent 1.333 reproduces Rainbow’s published 36.6; the article prints 1.33, which gives 36.9.

Hull speed and top speed

How fast a displacement hull goes

Hull speed = 1.34 × √LWL
Published the traditional rule
1.34 × √36.88.13 kt

The traditional rule ignores weight. Dave Gerr’s formula allows for it: light boats can be driven past 1.34 × √LWL without planing; heavy ones hold at it. It ignores sail area, and long overhangs make a boat’s D/L read high and her top speed low.

Top speed = 8.26 ÷ (D/L)^0.311 × √LWL never less than 1.34 × √LWL
Published Dave Gerr
Empty: 8.26 ÷ 272^0.311 × √36.88.8 kt
As she sails8.4 kt

Hull speed: Darrell Nicholson, “Determining a Fuel-efficient Engine RPM,” Practical Sailor, 2025. Top speed: Dave Gerr, “What Efficiency Means, How It Can Save Money and How It Makes for Better Boats,” Part 1, 2011 (gerrmarine.com). The calculator reproduces the worked example in Charles Doane, “Crunching Numbers: A Better Way to Estimate Hull Speed,” Wave Train, 2010: 7.9 kt for 12,000 lb on a 28 ft waterline.

Multihulls

Power, and the wind that lifts a hull

A multihull stands up on her beam, not her keel, so the ballast ratio, comfort ratio and capsize screening are left out. Three figures take their place. The worked figures are for a Lagoon 42, from the table in the source.

Bruce number = √sail area ÷ displacement^⅓
Wind to lift a hull = 9.8 × √(0.5 × BCL × displacement ÷ (sail area × HCOE)) BCL: beam between the hulls’ centerlines (overall beam less one hull’s beam) · HCOE: height of the sail plan’s center above the water · includes a 40% gust factor · flat water
Length to beam = LOA ÷ overall beam best range: 1.7 to 2.2 for a catamaran, 1.2 to 1.8 for a trimaran
Published all three
Bruce: √1,150 ÷ 16,550^⅓1.33
Wind to lift a hull: BCL 17.4 ft, HCOE 22.1 ft23.3 kt
Length to beam: 42 ÷ 221.91

An offshore cruising multihull should not need a reef below 22 to 25 kt apparent. Waves, and the wind getting under the bridgedeck once she heels, lower the real figure.

Sheet and halyard loads

What the sheet is holding

The load climbs with the square of the wind: twice the wind, four times the load. The halyard carries about the same. The mainsheet depends on where it meets the boom, so its figure is rough.

Load (lb) = sail area (ft²) × wind (kt)² × 0.00431 headsail area at LP: ½ × forestay × (J × LP) · line: breaking strength at least 5 × the load · winch: load ÷ a 40 lb pull

Range under power

How far the tanks take her

The power a displacement hull needs climbs steeply with speed. An efficient cruising speed is 60 to 70% of hull speed; past 75%, the fuel per mile climbs fast.

Speed-length ratio from power = 10.665 ÷ (displacement ÷ shaft hp)^⅓ shaft hp = 95% of rated · power at a speed: displacement × SL³ ÷ 1,213 · fuel: 0.054 gal/h per horsepower at the propeller · reserve: one-third
Published the fuel figure, the 95%, the efficient speed and the reserveOur estimate speed from power
SpeedPowerFuelRange
5.5 kt21 hp1.14 gal/h796 nm
6.1 kt29 hp1.57 gal/h645 nm
6.7 kt39 hp2.09 gal/h533 nm
8.4 kt, top76 hp4.10 gal/h340 nm

As she sails, 80 hp, 250 gal, a third kept in reserve. Her efficient speed is 4.9 to 5.7 kt. These figures assume calm water and a clean bottom.

The speed-from-power formula is widely attributed to Gerr, but we have not read it at its source, so it is our estimate here. Practical Sailor’s worked example uses Gerr’s propeller table, which needs the engine’s RPM. The formula here needs only horsepower and asks for about a third more power at cruising speed, so its ranges are on the short side.

Fuel, efficient speed and the rule of thirds: Darrell Nicholson, “Determining a Fuel-efficient Engine RPM,” Practical Sailor, 2025, quoting Dave Gerr. Shaft power: Dave Gerr, “What Efficiency Means,” Part 1, 2011: a direct shaft delivers 95 to 96% of the engine’s horsepower to the propeller.
